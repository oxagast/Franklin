Cookie theft, also known as session cookie hijacking or sidejacking, has been a known web application vulnerability and attack vector for over two decades. It involves the interception and misuse of session cookies, which are small pieces of data stored on a user's computer by a web browser. These cookies often contain session identifiers or authentication information, allowing users to remain logged in or maintain their session state across multiple page requests.

The earliest known public discussion of cookie theft as an attack vector dates back to the late 1990s, with one of the most notable instances being the "Cookie Cutter" attack presented by Mark S. Lottor at the Black Hat Briefings in 1998. This attack demonstrated how an attacker could steal session cookies from web applications and use them to impersonate users, gaining unauthorized access to their accounts or sensitive information.

To mitigate the risks associated with cookie theft, several encryption and protection mechanisms have been developed over the years:

1. Secure Flag: The Secure flag is set on cookies to ensure they are only transmitted over secure, encrypted connections using HTTPS. This prevents cookies from being intercepted during transmission.

2. HTTPOnly Flag: The HTTPOnly flag is used to restrict client-side script access to cookies, making them inaccessible to malicious scripts or cross-site scripting (XSS) attacks.

3. SameSite Attribute: The SameSite attribute, introduced in 2016, provides additional protection by restricting cookies from being sent in cross-site requests. It helps prevent cookies from being sent to malicious websites or during cross-site request forgery (CSRF) attacks.

     argue in favor of moral luck versus moral responsibility from Kants theories.

Moral luck is a concept that suggests that moral outcomes or evaluations can be attributed to chance or luck. For example, a person may be lucky and avoid causing harm in a situation, while another person facing similar circumstances might end up causing harm.

Kant's ethics, based on the notion of the categorical imperative, emphasizes duty and the responsibility of autonomous moral agents. According to Kant, morality arises from reason and rational choice, and individuals are expected to follow moral laws universally.

However, one could argue that Kant's theory, with its focus on individual autonomy and rational choice, may overlook circumstances or external factors that can influence moral outcomes. This is where the concept of moral luck could be introduced.

Moral luck, as a concept, highlights that moral responsibilities are not always solely determined by one's intentions or actions. It acknowledges that situations and contingencies beyond an individual's control can impact moral outcomes. This complements Kant's theory, which emphasizes the role of the agent's intentions.

For instance, consider two individuals, both of whom intend to help a person in distress but have differing outcomes due to circumstances beyond their control. The agent who succeeds in helping embodies the moral ideal and is praised for their actions. Meanwhile, the agent who fails, perhaps due to bad luck or unforeseen circumstances, is left questioning their moral responsibility.

Kant's theory, when combined with the concept of moral luck, could be viewed as supporting the idea that moral responsibility should be evaluated differently. Instead of solely focusing on the agent's intentions, this perspective acknowledges the role of luck or chance events in shaping moral outcomes. It encourages us to consider the circumstances and context in which moral actions are performed.
